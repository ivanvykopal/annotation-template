1.2 Manipulation techniques (part of the "disinformation" category)

For manipulative content, note which techniques the author used. There may be several in one article. Techniques are not selected from a list – they serve as an aid in recognizing manipulation. Justify your assessment in the justification field: ideally give a quotation from the article in which the technique is used, and an explanation of which technique you think it is and why the article is manipulative.

1. Cherry Picking

Presenting only the data that support a thesis/narrative while ignoring the broader context. It concerns mainly data (most often statistics) taken out of context, as well as one-sided selection of arguments in which the author omits the positive or negative sides of the matter. Personal experience belongs under Anecdote, not Cherry Picking.

  • Example 1: “The data show that last winter was the coldest in the last 10 years, so the climate is not warming at all.” — A single data point taken out of context; the long-term trend (decades) clearly shows warming.
  • Example 2: “Stalin transformed the USSR into a strong state with developed industry, mechanized agriculture and education available to all. His influence on the development of Russia and the Soviet republics was exceptionally positive.” — The author completely omits the negative sides of Stalin's rule and thus substantially distorts historical truth.

Annotation note: in the justification quote at least one sentence using the technique; for example 2 several sentences need to be quoted.

2. Quote Mining

Using a short excerpt from someone else's longer speech in a way that significantly distorts its original tone or meaning.

  • Example 1: “It is as if the fossils were simply planted there, without any evolutionary history.” — An excerpt from R. Dawkins' book The Blind Watchmaker used by creationists as proof of their belief. In the original text, however, Dawkins criticizes creationists and rejects their hypothesis.
  • Example 2: “I hate Poles.” — A part of the sentence was cut out which completely reversed its meaning. The original sentence read: “I would never say that I hate Poles.”

Annotation note: usually one sentence is enough; sometimes the context requires several sentences.

3. Anecdote

Using a personal experience, an isolated case, or hearsay as evidence, most often to question statistics.

  • Example 1: “Exactly 25 cm of snow fell in Orava. Global warming is the biggest lie in the history of mankind.” — A single personal experience cannot be transferred to the whole population or to multi-year studies.
  • Example 2: “My uncle, who works in a hospital, said that many people died because of COVID-19 vaccines.” — The claim is unverifiable: it is impossible to find out whether the relative really said it, or whether he works in a relevant place and has knowledge of the matter.

Annotation note: usually one sentence is enough.

4. Whataboutism

Responding to a factual argument not by addressing it but by raising a new, unrelated issue. It may be a false trail to divert attention (Red Herring) or an appeal to hypocrisy (Tu quoque – “you too”): the opponent has something on their conscience as well, and therefore has no right to criticize.

  • Example 1: “You talk about the persecution of LGBT+ people. What about hungry children? Nobody thinks of them!” — The author shifts attention to another, usually ideologically neutral problem and thereby conceals their own bias.
  • Example 2: “What about all the Clintons' ties to Russia, including the Podesta firm, the uranium deal, the Russian “reset”, speaking fees, etc.?” — The accusation of ties to Russia is deflected by pointing out that the opponent has them too (Red Herring).
  • Example 3: “If NATO could bomb Serbia in 1999, Russia can bomb Ukraine in 2022.” — Tu quoque: accusing the other side of hypocrisy serves as a justification of one's own actions.

Annotation note: usually one or two sentences are enough.

5. Strawman

Distorting someone else's argument so that it is easier to refute. Usually this means attributing to the opponent a position they do not hold at all.

  • Example 1: “If you support LGBT, you also support pedophilia = zero respect, no tolerance.” — The author claims that supporting one thing means supporting another, universally condemned thing, and thus portrays the opponent in the worst light.
  • Example 2: “If you criticize Russia's actions, you are a Russophobe.” — Criticism is equated with prejudice, which makes it possible to portray oneself or someone else as a victim.

Annotation note: usually one sentence is enough.

6. Suggestive questions

Flooding the recipient with a series of leading questions that steer them towards a predetermined thesis. The author often appears neutral (“just asking questions”) while directing the reader's attitude (usually negatively).

  • Example 1: “I think we don't even know the real mortality of monkeypox. Has a Westerner ever died of it? Is it even the same disease as in Africa? If so, how could it appear in so many countries at once? (…) Doesn't this just make us need another dose?” — The questions lead to a certain conclusion; the author avoids the accusation of spreading conspiracies and can always defend themselves by saying they are “just asking”.
  • Example 2: “Is it a coincidence that the war in Ukraine broke out right when the pandemic ended? And that it immediately became the main media topic? Doesn't it mean that elites are trying to hide something with another big event to distract our attention?” — The questions have an obvious thesis and also aim to evoke negative emotions.

Annotation note: quote at least three suggestive questions. They need not follow one another or come from one part of the text, but they must clearly present a specific thesis.

7. Appeal to emotion

Using words and phrases meant to evoke a strong emotion and attitude in the recipient. Subtypes: appeal to fear/prejudice, appeal to values and traditions, slogans (short strong phrases with stereotypes or labeling), emotionally loaded language (insulting and hateful), Flag Waving (appeal to group membership) and appeal to time (a call to act now).

  • Example 1 (emotionally loaded language): “Our children are being MURDERED by vaccines! They are DEVILS who want to hurt the innocent! All doctors who vaccinate will face cruel punishment.” — The highlighted keywords evoke negative emotions and designate the culprits.
  • Example 2 (appeal to fear and prejudice): “If we let migrants into the country, they will rob us and we will be afraid to go out on the street.” — A simple, frightening and oversimplified vision based on social prejudices.
  • Example 3 (appeal to values): “Women have always taken care of the household and children, not a career. If our ancestors did it, there is no point in changing it.” — An appeal to tradition; often accompanied by phrases such as “everybody thinks so” or “nobody disagrees”.
  • Example 4 (slogan): “Slovakia first! We will give nothing to anyone!” — A short catchy slogan referring to prejudices that is hard to challenge.
  • Example 5 (emotionally loaded language / hate speech): “Every gay person is a pedophile, so they must be registered and kept in separate camps.” — Arguments based on hatred and radical prejudices, often amounting to hate speech.
  • Example 6 (flag waving): “We who fight against Big Pharma know very well that vaccines are dangerous, and we do not need studies by corrupt scientists for that, because every reasonable person can see it.” — The author assumes the audience shares their views and is content to point to group membership.
  • Example 7 (appeal to time): “The time has come to take to the streets and stand up to the rainbow dictatorship.” — Mobilizing the audience to act without factual arguments.

Annotation note: usually one sentence is enough.

8. False cause

Assuming a causal relationship solely on the basis of an observed correlation. This includes assuming that two simultaneous events are related (Cum hoc ergo propter hoc), or that an event following another is its consequence (Post hoc ergo propter hoc).

  • Example 1: “Russia's military intervention in Ukraine is the culmination of US and NATO aggression that began with their blitzkrieg against Serbia 23 years ago.” — A marked oversimplification: the author picks a convenient cause and omits everything else.
  • Example 2 (Cum hoc): “We started vaccinating against COVID-19 and that is exactly when a new wave of illness began. It looks like vaccines infect more effectively than the virus itself.” — The simultaneous occurrence of two events does not mean that one causes the other.
  • Example 3 (Post hoc): “A new government was elected a month ago and we already have an economic collapse.” — Causality is assumed merely from temporal sequence; many phenomena have several complex causes that accumulate over months or years.

Annotation note: usually one sentence is enough.

9. Exaggeration

Simplifying and distorting a phenomenon or problem. Subtypes: Slippery Slope (one decision leads to a chain of undesirable consequences), Blowfish (inflating marginal aspects or the views of individuals to smear a whole group), Magnified Minority (overestimating the importance of a small group with a different opinion) and Oversimplification.

  • Example 1 (Blowfish): “I just read an article about one of the LGBT+ activists being a pedophile. That is perfect proof that the whole community wants to harm children!” — An isolated case is used to smear the whole community.
  • Example 2 (Slippery Slope): “If we allow same-sex marriage, the next step will be the legalization of pedophilia.” — Falsely portrayed consequences of a decision meant to evoke fear or aversion.
  • Example 3 (Magnified Minority): “This scientist claims that global warming is a lie. They expelled him from the community for telling the truth.” — The opinion of a narrow group is used to blur the real picture of the community.
  • Example 4 (Oversimplification): “Roma commit more crimes, which means they are by nature more prone to violence.” — The author maximally simplifies a complex phenomenon to confirm their prejudices and ignores the many influencing factors.

Annotation note: usually one sentence is enough.

10. False reference

Referring to unreliable sources or persons: passing on knowledge from anonymous people (e.g., from social media), citing propaganda claims of politicians or media (especially from authoritarian states), or using false quotes circulating on the internet. False experts or other people posing as an authority are often used (Appeal to Authority).

  • Example 1: “User @Xbv3321 analyzed the studies that were supposed to “prove” the effectiveness of COVID-19 vaccines. He clearly proved that they are a lie and that Big Pharma is deceiving people.” — An appeal to an anonymous authority whose competence cannot be assessed.
  • Example 2: “Vladimir Putin said that Western sanctions against Russia do not work and the economy is only growing. Putin is again proving what a good president he is, and the West has shown its weakness.” — A politician's propaganda claims are treated as a reliable source.
  • Example 3: “Dostoevsky already knew that neo-Marxism would destroy us: “Tolerance will reach such a level that intelligent people will be forbidden to think so as not to offend the fools.” That is exactly how woke and cancel culture are trying to destroy traditional values.” — A false quote that Dostoevsky never said; the author exploits the authority of a famous person.
  • Example 4 (Appeal to Authority): “Former Pfizer vice president Michael Yeadon warns that pharmaceutical companies are lying! He said that vaccines have not been properly tested and are extremely dangerous.” — A person posing as an expert lends credibility to the spread claims, although they are a marginal member of the community, often excluded from scientific institutions.

Annotation note: usually one or two sentences are enough.

Manipulation outside the listed techniques

If you think you have found manipulation that cannot be assigned to any of the listed techniques, describe it in the assessment justification field: give a quotation from the analyzed text and briefly explain why you think it is manipulation.

STEP II – A detailed analysis of the content

2.1 Classification within a group of disinformation (exhaustive list, multiple choice)

  • Falseness/fabrication — all explicitly false content and articles containing fabricated documents/data/quotes and the like. Articles classified in this group require a fact-checking comment. The analyst's task is to identify explicitly false or fabricated content, and then to find arguments/sources refuting the false statement. Selecting this option allows you to quote a false statement in the text box. The second text box requires a link to an article providing evidence refuting the quoted statement (in particular scientific sources and articles from reliable organizations dealing with disinformation study).

Note: For help in finding verified disinformation claims, it is best to first go to our developed tool: Fact-check finder or Google Fact Check Tools. If you are unable to find a fact-checking article, simply post an article that contains credible data that can be used to refute those used in the disinformation article. When citing independent NGOs, pay special attention to whether the organization is affiliated with the International Fact-Checking Network (IFCN). Both text boxes (the quoted false statement and the refuting link) are required.

  • Manipulation — bending or distorting facts in order to prove one's point or to influence someone else's views and behavior. Manipulative articles are often not explicitly false, but they contain incomplete statements, used in an inappropriate context, selected information, etc. Try to identify all manipulation techniques in the article. Of course, an article may also cover just one technique. Examples of manipulation techniques can be found in the Manipulation techniques - examples and definitions document.
  • Trolling/satire — posting controversial, often untrue content, showing certain phenomena in a caricatured, playful manner. Disinformation articles, including trolling/satire, can be based on both falseness and manipulation. After selecting this option, an analysis adequate to the type of disinformation should be carried out: falseness/fabrication, manipulation, or both.
  • Conspiracy theory — proposed explanation of an event assuming significant participation of a group of conspirators trying to hide the truth from the public. To exist, a conspiracy theory needs an official version of a given event, which the conspiracy theory refutes by suggesting an alternative version in its place. In order for an article to meet the definition of a conspiracy theory, it must meet most of the traits included in CONSPIR.

Choosing this category exempts the evaluation of the manipulation techniques used and any fact-checking.

Note: Choosing conspiracy theory means that we do not choose manipulation or falseness/fabrication, because we assume that conspiracy theories use too many manipulation techniques and use such an amount of falseness that it is impossible to verify it quickly.

STEP III – Interpretation of motivation and intentions

3.1 Motivation (exhaustive list, multiple choice)

Note: Motivation answers the question: why is someone spreading disinformation?

  • Internal political — internal political motivations are characterized by a strong marking of content against one political option. They can be directed against both a political minority (opposition) and the majority (currently in power). Typical of this type of motivation is a clear definition of one's political group (an affiliation of the author/author's views), which allows for indicating their motivation.
  • External political — motivated in an external political manner is usually any kind of propaganda that weakens international alliances (such as the EU or NATO), as well as ridicules or weakens the defense capabilities of a given country. Typical of this type of disinformation is its promotion by authors who have connections with foreign countries. An additional premise indicating the above-mentioned motivation is the promotion of content that supports the demands of foreign countries. Note: The author of the disinformation does not need to be aware that he is reproducing propaganda that is being distributed by outside influences.
  • Economic/Financial — in the case of this type of disinformation, it is characteristic to promote among recipients specific products (e.g., encouraging the purchase of a specific supplement/drug under an article that incorrectly informs about its effectiveness) or by encouraging contributions to the activities of an organization/author disseminating disinformative content.
  • Gaining popularity — several authors disseminate false content in order to gain popularity. This motivation can occur both in the case of misinformation, and disinformation. This motivation can also be recognized by the fact of publishing false content under one's own name. It also happens that false information is disseminated for this reason on Internet forums/blogospheres as well, in order to gain an appropriate position within a given environment.
  • Social — social motivation is extremely important in the case of disinformation because it is based on the formation of groups and opposition to them, creating a division of "us/them". This motivation often comes down to antagonizing the society, confirming or reinforcing the erroneous views of a given group or falsely refuting the arguments of opponents. Its aim is also to strengthen faith in the false beliefs of one social group.
  • Chosen one/Savior complex — ego building, willingness to make people aware of the 'truth', sharing false information due to fear or author's internal need for the existence of simple explanations. This motivation is typical of authors of conspiracy theories. Usually, it involves recognizing the existence of a (false) conspiracy, consisting in accusing public institutions or scientists of concealing the truth. Note: disinformation cannot be based solely on this motivation. If we have selected disinformation, in addition to the Chosen one/Savior complex option, another must be selected as the main one.

3.2 Intention (exhaustive list, multiple choice)

Note: It is in this category that we try to answer the question: what is the purpose of spreading disinformation by a particular author?

  • Undermining the credibility of public institutions — the goal of many disinformation authors is to destroy trust in public institutions. This can be done by undermining official communications, insinuating bad intentions or falsely exposing corruption (e.g., accusing governments of population control with vaccines).
  • Changing political views — influencing voter preferences is a common procedure used by disinformation authors. Changing political beliefs is aimed at strengthening one side of a political dispute and arousing resentment against the others. It usually involves the simultaneous promotion of politicians from extremist movements, which are treated as an alternative to the major parties.
  • Undermining international organizations and alliances — undermining the credibility of international institutions is often part of disinformation activities carried out by external forces (e.g., Russia). These are aimed at breaking up alliances of democratic states to facilitate propaganda efforts by authoritarian states. Of course, numerous extreme political movements also have an interest in shattering trust in international institutions. This is part of a populist influence on society and a way to gain power. International institutions are then most frequently portrayed as entities that take away the sovereignty of member states.
  • Promoting social stereotypes/antagonisms — deepening social divisions is a frequent goal of disinformation efforts. A strongly divided society is less resistant to manipulation, and mutual distrust also promotes a collapse of confidence in the institution of the state and democracy. This causes internal problems to absorb most of the attention, giving room for external centers of influence to operate. This can take the form of reinforcing xenophobia or aversion to specific social groups.
  • Promoting anti-scientific views — science is a frequent enemy of disinformation authors. Science enhances critical thinking and is an important part of the strength of Western democracies. Presenting it as an enemy aids in undermining the system under which Western countries operate. Reinforcing anti-scientific attitudes also enables short-term financial gain. The fight against science can be based on a direct attack on scientists, but is also a significant element of conspiracy theories.

3.3 Evoked emotions (exhaustive list, multiple choice)

Note: This is the most subjective part of the evaluation. It involves assessing what emotions the author wants to evoke in his or her audience. The easiest way is to put yourself in the shoes of a person who believes in a given disinformation and, from their perspective, assess what emotions a given article would evoke in this hypothetical situation.

Fear/sense of threat · Anger · Opposition/rebellion · Uncertainty/sense of confusion · Hope (false hope) · Pride · Contempt · Indifference
