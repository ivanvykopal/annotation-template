How to perform an article evaluation

STEP I – Overall assessment

{{#FULL}}

1.1 Author analysis (exhaustive list, single choice)

Note: the author's rating does not affect the assessment of the nature of the article (e.g., disinformation/credible, etc.).

  • Anonymous — there is no information enabling the identification of the author (i.e., there is no information about the author or there are only unknown initials).
  • Unknown author — personal data provided does not allow for a broader identification of the person or the data may be potentially fabricated (only name and surname).
  • Known author — content signed with personal data, enabling a wider identification of the author (you can easily, for example, by clicking on the name, get more information about such a person).
{{/FULL}}

1.2 Content analysis (exhaustive list, single choice)

Note: This step requires analyzing the entire content of the article and recognizing whether the information is true or of disinformative nature.

  • Credible information — article provides only factual information, presents verifiable content, cites the sources from which it gets information and uses neutral language. Selecting this category ends the assessment of the article.
  • Disinformation — all forms of false, inaccurate, or misleading information reproduced, regardless of the author's intentions. In most cases they are designed, presented, and promoted to intentionally cause public harm or for profit. The detected information must be verifiable, which means that it can be proved that it is untrue or manipulation techniques were used.
  • Hard to say — content is not verifiable. Selecting this category ends the assessment of the article.
  • Inconsistent with the topic — the article is not relevant to the topic at hand or does not display properly. Selecting this category ends the assessment of the article.

Note: Selecting item 1, 3 or 4 ends the evaluation of the article. Therefore, read the article carefully and make sure that it meets the criteria for marking any of the categories listed above.

STEP II – A detailed analysis of the content

2.1 Classification within a group of disinformation (exhaustive list, multiple choice)

  • Falseness/fabrication — all explicitly false content and articles containing fabricated documents/data/quotes and the like. Articles classified in this group require a fact-checking comment. The analyst's task is to identify explicitly false or fabricated content, and then to find arguments/sources refuting the false statement. Selecting this option allows you to quote a false statement in the text box. The second text box requires a link to an article providing evidence refuting the quoted statement (in particular scientific sources and articles from reliable organizations dealing with disinformation study).

Note: For help in finding verified disinformation claims, it is best to first go to Google Fact Check Tools. If you are unable to find a fact-checking article, simply post an article that contains credible data that can be used to refute those used in the disinformation article.

  • Manipulation — bending or distorting facts in order to prove one's point or to influence someone else's views and behavior. Manipulative articles are often not explicitly false, but they contain incomplete statements, used in an inappropriate context, selected information, etc. Try to identify all manipulation techniques in the article. Of course, an article may also cover just one technique. Examples of manipulation techniques can be found in the Manipulation techniques - examples and definitions document.
  • Trolling/satire — posting controversial, often untrue content, showing certain phenomena in a caricatured, playful manner. Disinformation articles, including trolling/satire, can be based on both falseness and manipulation. After selecting this option, an analysis adequate to the type of disinformation should be carried out: falseness/fabrication, manipulation, or both.
  • Conspiracy theory — proposed explanation of an event assuming significant participation of a group of conspirators trying to hide the truth from the public. To exist, a conspiracy theory needs an official version of a given event, which the conspiracy theory refutes by suggesting an alternative version in its place. In order for an article to meet the definition of a conspiracy theory, it must meet most of the traits included in CONSPIR.

Choosing this category exempts the evaluation of the manipulation techniques used and any fact-checking.

Note: Choosing conspiracy theory means that we do not choose manipulation or falseness/fabrication, because we assume that conspiracy theories use too many manipulation techniques and use such an amount of falseness that it is impossible to verify it quickly.

STEP III – Interpretation of motivation and intentions

3.1 Motivation (exhaustive list, multiple choice)

Note: Motivation answers the question: why is someone spreading disinformation?

  • Internal political — internal political motivations are characterized by a strong marking of content against one political option. They can be directed against both a political minority (opposition) and the majority (currently in power). Typical of this type of motivation is a clear definition of one's political group (an affiliation of the author/author's views), which allows for indicating their motivation.
  • External political — motivated in an external political manner is usually any kind of propaganda that weakens international alliances (such as the EU or NATO), as well as ridicules or weakens the defense capabilities of a given country. Typical of this type of disinformation is its promotion by authors who have connections with foreign countries. An additional premise indicating the above-mentioned motivation is the promotion of content that supports the demands of foreign countries. Note: The author of the disinformation does not need to be aware that he is reproducing propaganda that is being distributed by outside influences.
  • Economic/Financial — in the case of this type of disinformation, it is characteristic to promote among recipients specific products (e.g., encouraging the purchase of a specific supplement/drug under an article that incorrectly informs about its effectiveness) or by encouraging contributions to the activities of an organization/author disseminating disinformative content.
  • Gaining popularity — several authors disseminate false content in order to gain popularity. This motivation can occur both in the case of misinformation, and disinformation. This motivation can also be recognized by the fact of publishing false content under one's own name. It also happens that false information is disseminated for this reason on Internet forums/blogospheres as well, in order to gain an appropriate position within a given environment.
  • Social — social motivation is extremely important in the case of disinformation because it is based on the formation of groups and opposition to them, creating a division of "us/them". This motivation often comes down to antagonizing the society, confirming or reinforcing the erroneous views of a given group or falsely refuting the arguments of opponents. Its aim is also to strengthen faith in the false beliefs of one social group.
  • Chosen one/Savior complex — ego building, willingness to make people aware of the 'truth', sharing false information due to fear or author's internal need for the existence of simple explanations. This motivation is typical of authors of conspiracy theories. Usually, it involves recognizing the existence of a (false) conspiracy, consisting in accusing public institutions or scientists of concealing the truth. Note: disinformation cannot be based solely on this motivation. If we have selected disinformation, in addition to the Chosen one/Savior complex option, another must be selected as the main one.

3.2 Intention (exhaustive list, multiple choice)

Note: It is in this category that we try to answer the question: what is the purpose of spreading disinformation by a particular author?

  • Undermining the credibility of public institutions — the goal of many disinformation authors is to destroy trust in public institutions. This can be done by undermining official communications, insinuating bad intentions or falsely exposing corruption (e.g., accusing governments of population control with vaccines).
  • Changing political views — influencing voter preferences is a common procedure used by disinformation authors. Changing political beliefs is aimed at strengthening one side of a political dispute and arousing resentment against the others. It usually involves the simultaneous promotion of politicians from extremist movements, which are treated as an alternative to the major parties.
  • Undermining international organizations and alliances — undermining the credibility of international institutions is often part of disinformation activities carried out by external forces (e.g., Russia). These are aimed at breaking up alliances of democratic states to facilitate propaganda efforts by authoritarian states. Of course, numerous extreme political movements also have an interest in shattering trust in international institutions. This is part of a populist influence on society and a way to gain power. International institutions are then most frequently portrayed as entities that take away the sovereignty of member states.
  • Promoting social stereotypes/antagonisms — deepening social divisions is a frequent goal of disinformation efforts. A strongly divided society is less resistant to manipulation, and mutual distrust also promotes a collapse of confidence in the institution of the state and democracy. This causes internal problems to absorb most of the attention, giving room for external centers of influence to operate. This can take the form of reinforcing xenophobia or aversion to specific social groups.
  • Promoting anti-scientific views — science is a frequent enemy of disinformation authors. Science enhances critical thinking and is an important part of the strength of Western democracies. Presenting it as an enemy aids in undermining the system under which Western countries operate. Reinforcing anti-scientific attitudes also enables short-term financial gain. The fight against science can be based on a direct attack on scientists, but is also a significant element of conspiracy theories.

3.3 Evoked emotions (exhaustive list, multiple choice)

Note: This is the most subjective part of the evaluation. It involves assessing what emotions the author wants to evoke in his or her audience. The easiest way is to put yourself in the shoes of a person who believes in a given disinformation and, from their perspective, assess what emotions a given article would evoke in this hypothetical situation.

Fear/sense of threat · Anger · Opposition/rebellion · Uncertainty/sense of confusion · Hope (false hope) · Pride · Contempt · Indifference

STEP IV – Narratives

4.1 Narratives (exhaustive list, multiple choice)

Note: Narratives are intended to answer the question: how does a given author reproduce disinformation?

The narratives are divided by topic. Each of them contains 5 statements each. The selection is limited only to narratives assigned to a specific topic. All narratives can be found in the Narratives document.
